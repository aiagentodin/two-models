Does "flush, wick, recover" predict anything?

Your APLD note asked whether a heavy selling week, then a new-low wick and a strong recovery week, tends to lead to a lasting rise. We tested that exact shape on every US stock since 2005. Short answer: no. The stocks that made it did about the same as similar stocks that didn't. APLD was one of the lucky ones.

How to read it. Out of 90 numbers (9 versions × 5 holding periods × 2 comparisons), 3 clear zero. Pure chance would give about 4 or 5 of the 90. The best was "both extras together, compared with similar bounces, held 5 weeks": +1.2 points. But that came almost entirely from older years: +2.2 in 2005–12, +2.4 in 2013–19 and +0.2 since 2020. It rests on only 2,055 cases, and buying and selling costs would take a good part of it.

The deep-flush version did worse than its matches (about −1.5 to −2 points at 20–40 weeks). That's the opposite of what a "capitulation" story predicts.

Why compare with "similar stocks"?

A stock that just fell hard and bounced is unusual in a few ways. It tends to be jumpy (volatile), it's often in a downtrend, and it's moving with the whole market that week. Any of those can change what happens next, with no candle pattern involved. So for each case we compared it with every other stock in the same week that matched it on those traits but didn't show the pattern. If the pattern carries real information, it has to beat that group.

We used two comparison groups. "Similar stocks" matches trend, jumpiness, six-month performance and how heavily the stock trades. "Similar bounce" swaps six-month performance for the stock's last two weeks, so it also fell and bounced. That second group asks the sharper question: does the shape of the candles add anything beyond an ordinary drop and bounce?

APLD: your numbers check out against our prices: an 8.4% down week, the $20.00 low on 30 March, and about 23% more volume in the recovery week. It then had a great run. But it was one of 11,398 APLD-like cases since 2005, and on average those did only about half a point better than their matches at 10 weeks. More than half of them did worse.

BWXT: in our prices BWXT rose 1.4% in the week of 23–27 March, so it wasn't a flush that week. The −10% and +15.7% figures in the note may come from different dates, so they're worth re-checking. From the same April date it fell 28% over 20 weeks.

Two examples can't settle a question like this. A pattern that appears about 1,200 times a year will always give you a few spectacular charts to point at. The only fair test is to look at all of them.

Answers to the questions in your note

Which indicators turn first?

Earlier this month we ran the same kind of test on 19 daily indicator signals: RSI crossing back above 30, a MACD cross, a stochastic cross up from below 20, ADX above 25, on-balance volume at a new high, three Bollinger Band signals, moving-average crosses, a volume surge, and the TTM Squeeze. The test covered Nasdaq stocks from 2010 to 2026, including ones that later delisted, each compared with matched stocks bought the same day. None was more than 0.3 points better or worse than its match over the next 20 trading days. A wider rerun of the squeeze on all three exchanges from 2005 agreed. With no edge in the pattern and none in the indicators, there's no early warning to find. (Chaikin Money Flow wasn't in that set.)

Does volume confirmation help?

Not in a way we can measure. "More volume in the recovery week" nudged the average up by a few tenths of a point, but every range still crosses zero, and from 2013 to 2019 it was negative at 40 weeks. A real volume surge (1.5× the stock's 10-week average) added nothing.

How often does it fail?

More often than it works. In every version, only 42–49% of cases beat their matches.

What about 5, 10, 20 and 40 weeks out?

All in the chart above; use the "Held for" buttons. No holding period works.

The fine print

How the test was run

Daily prices for every US common stock on the NYSE, Nasdaq and NYSE American from 2005 to November 2026, including companies that later went bust or were bought out. Leaving those out makes every buy-the-dip idea look better than it is. Prices are adjusted for splits and dividends. Weeks run Monday to Friday. Stocks had to be at least $5 and trade at least $5 million a day, so penny stocks are out and APLD-sized names are in.

The trade: buy at the next Monday's opening price after the recovery week, then measure the total return 1, 5, 10, 20 and 40 weeks later. The likely ranges come from resampling whole months (a bootstrap), so a single wild month can't dominate. Results are before trading costs, which would only lower them. Nothing was tuned after seeing results.

What it doesn't cover: stocks under $5, and short selling. The same shape built from daily rather than weekly candles wasn't tested; the daily-indicator tests above cover the daily signals in your checklist.

This is a backtest: what a fixed rule would have done on past prices. It is not investment advice. The price data comes from a paid vendor whose licence doesn't allow passing the raw files on. The test code and the summary results can be shared on request.
