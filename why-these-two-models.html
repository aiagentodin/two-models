How 36,124 tested ideas came down to two

Since February 2026 the project has tested 36,124 versions of investing ideas on 25 years of US market history. That history includes every company that later went bust and only the information public on each day. Every idea had to pass the same tests, built to catch winners that were only lucky. Two systems got through: the 50/30/20 stock book and the A1 crypto rule. We run them together.

What this page shows, and what it doesn't. It shows that these two are the strongest of everything we tested, under rules built to catch false winners. It does not claim they can't lose. The limits section sets out the 2026 drawdown and everything else a careful reader should weigh.

1 · The data

No hindsight, and no survivors-only history

Most backtests that look too good fail on the data, before any statistics. Two errors cause most of it, and we built the data to prevent both.

Only what was known that day

A company's numbers for a quarter become public weeks after the quarter ends. A careless test uses them from the quarter's end and gains weeks of hindsight. Our data stamps every report with the day it was filed (as first reported, never the later corrected figures). A report filed after the market closes counts from the next morning.

Every company, including the dead ones

A test run only on companies that exist today never buys a future bankruptcy, so almost any rule looks good. This is called survivorship bias. Our history keeps 18,199 US companies, including the 70% that later went bust, were bought out, or were delisted.

We measured the damage on our own early work. A champion strategy tested on a survivors-only list showed about 53% a year. On the full list it made 25–34%. That finding is why the full list became mandatory.

2 · The tests

Ten checks built to kill false winners

Every candidate faced the same sequence. Most failed somewhere along it, which is the point.

1

Write the test before seeing the result

Each test's plan, pass bar and data are sealed first, with a digital fingerprint saved in the project history. The result can't move the goalposts.

515sealed plans
2

Use only what was knowable that day

Point-in-time data, every dead company included, trades filled at the next day's open, real trading costs and top-bracket taxes.

next opentrade fill price
3

Discount for the number of tries

Test 1,000 ideas and some will look brilliant by luck. Every verdict is marked down for the full count of attempts in its family (the Deflated Sharpe test).

272verdicts discounted
4

Check for curve-fitting

History is cut into 8 blocks and recombined 70 ways. If the in-sample winner usually ranks below the middle on the held-out blocks, it is rejected.

70splits per test
5

Beat luck, not just the market

Results are compared with reshuffled copies of history and with random stock picks. The momentum leg beat all 150 random portfolios: 23.1% a year against a random median of 15.3%.

~29Mreshuffled histories
6

Compare at equal risk

An idea must beat simply holding less of the market at the same risk. Of 106 sell-trigger rules, 0 beat plain volatility sizing.

0 / 106triggers survived
7

Test on history it never saw

Promising rules are re-run on eras kept out of development: the French stock market 1940–2000 and the Nasdaq 1972–2000.

2unseen eras
8

Scan for leaks every night

A separate checker looks for 7 kinds of look-ahead leak. Known leaky test cases are re-run nightly and must still be caught.

7leak types checked
9

Prove it forward, on paper

Survivors run as paper accounts from a fixed date, judged by a pre-set statistical test at day 180 and day 365.

14forward cards
10

Close dead ends for good

When a family of ideas fails, re-skins of it are refused automatically unless they bring genuinely new information.

4 · The two

What survived, and why these two

stocks · since 2001

50/30/20 book

  1. 50%: 15 US stocks with the strongest 6–12-month price trend, sized to a volatility target, each sold if it falls 20% from its high.
  2. 30%: the Nasdaq-100 at 3× (TQQQ). 20%: the chip index at 3× (SOXL).
  3. Each part is invested only while its market is above its 200-day average, and exposure is cut when near-term fear (VIX) runs above three-month fear (VIX3M).
  4. Idle money goes to a trend sleeve of stocks, bonds, gold, oil and the dollar, each held only in an up-trend.
crypto · since 2018

A1 crypto rule

  1. Bitcoin 70%, ether 30%, each held only while its own last-30-day return is positive.
  2. Everything goes to cash if bitcoin is below its 200-day average or more than 25% below its 100-day high.
  3. Sized to a volatility target, decided once a week, run through Interactive Brokers.
  4. Idle money goes to the same trend sleeve.

Why run them together

The two systems rarely lose at the same time, so together they fall less than either alone. Alone, the 50/30/20 has a worst fall deeper than our signed −40% limit. Combined with A1 (60% A1, 40% 50/30/20) the backtest made 39.7% a year with a worst fall of −28.3% (Sharpe 1.20, 2018–26). Even the harsher reshuffled-history estimate, −35%, stays inside the limit.

The limit they were chosen against

Signed on 22 Sep 2026: the whole portfolio's worst fall must stay within −40%, in the backtest and in the reshuffled-history estimate. A breach of up to 5 points is allowed only if it buys at least 2 points a year of growth and passes further checks. Anything deeper is refused. Every candidate in this report was scored against that one rule.

5 · Limits

What a careful reader should weigh

The 2026 drawdown is real

The 50/30/20 paper account fell 47.9% from its 22 June peak to 5 November, deeper than anything in its 25-year backtest. It is down 35.8% since 29 May (about 42% below the peak on 11 Nov), while the S&P 500 is up 1.2%. Money moved out of tech and chip stocks while the overall index stayed flat. That is the worst kind of market for this book, and its 200-day rule never triggered. Reshuffled history says a fall this deep appears in about two of three 20-year runs, but one this fast is rare (0.3%). This is why the book is not run alone.

What the numbers do not yet prove

Most results ran on history the research had already seen; only a handful of sealed tests used fresh eras. A1 carries a 7-point allowance for this. Crypto history is short: on 2013–15 bitcoin the most aggressive A1 setting fell 46%, and ether's edge came mostly in 2020–21. The figures are before tax: after tax at a top-bracket rate, the 50/30/20 is about 15–17% a year and A1 about 28%. Forward records are days to months long; A1's first scheduled checkpoint is 29 April 2027.

Data and method limits

The sealed feature store guarantees which reports were used on each date, but not that every value matches the first-published number: no vendor keeps original values once revised. Adjusted prices use today's split and dividend factors, which is standard and does not change returns. Large foreign companies joined the universe only on 22 Sep 2026, so earlier tests used a narrower list. The nightly leak checker catches structural leaks, not every possible one. About half of verdicts had too few configurations for the curve-fitting test to apply, and 31 that scored badly on it were flagged rather than automatically demoted. Leverage costs at full size are estimated from broker what-if quotes, not yet from live trading.

Glossary

Terms used on this page

Backtest
Running a rule on past prices to see what it would have done.
Point-in-time (PIT)
Data stored as it was known on each date, so a test can't use facts published later.
Survivorship bias
Testing only on companies that still exist today. It quietly drops every bankruptcy and makes almost any stock-picking rule look better.
Growth per year (CAGR)
The steady yearly rate that turns the starting amount into the ending amount.
Worst fall (max drawdown)
The biggest drop from a high point to a later low.
Sharpe ratio
Return earned per unit of up-and-down movement. Higher is better; the S&P 500 is about 0.5 over 2001–26.
Deflated Sharpe / true N
A Sharpe test marked down for how many ideas were tried (N), so a lucky winner out of thousands fails.
PBO
Probability of backtest overfitting: how often the best in-sample version disappoints on held-out history.
Sealed plan (preregistration)
The test written and fingerprinted before running it.
Reshuffled history (bootstrap)
Many re-ordered copies of past returns, used to see how much a result depends on luck.
3× fund
A fund that moves three times the daily move of its index (TQQQ, SOXL).
200-day average
The average price over the last 200 trading days; above it counts as an up-trend.
VIX / VIX3M
Market fear gauges for the next month and next three months.
Volatility target
Sizing a position so its expected swings stay near a set level.
Paper card
A tracked account with no real money, recording a system's live results from a fixed start date.
